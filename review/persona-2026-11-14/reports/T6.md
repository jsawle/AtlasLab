# T6 Grace: middle school Earth science. Review of School Atlas 5.0.0-beta.43

Judged only from the packet (notes, screenshots 01 to 27, facts). The earthquake map reviewed is "Exploring earthquakes and their causes (Guided Lesson)". Some of what I flag is the map's own content (pop-up wording, layer names) and not the app's tools; I say so where I can tell, because to me at the projector it is all one thing.

## Findings, most serious first

### 1. The Legend is empty on the earthquake map
- **Place:** 12-tool-legend.png, Legend panel, top right. Also 06-map-opened.png, the whole map.
- **What is there:** The Legend panel opens with the title "Legend" and nothing under it. Meanwhile the map shows yellow, turquoise, grey and red dots of different sizes, some with rings, plus thick white lines.
- **Why it matters to me:** I cannot tell students what a colour or a dot size means. Is red "big", "deep" or "recent"? Is size magnitude? Without a key I would be guessing in front of the class, and students will invent their own meaning (most likely "red = dangerous place"). Magnitude is also not a simple scale (each whole number is about 32 times the energy), so dot size without a key invites the wrong comparison.
- **How serious:** Stops the lesson.
- **Suggested change:** Make the Legend show every layer that is switched on, with its colours and sizes. If the legend was only slow to load when the picture was taken, show "Loading..." so it never looks blank. **Needs checking in the live app**: the Map layers panel has small arrows beside "Earthquakes (Last 30 Days)" and "Tectonic Plate Boundaries" that may open a key there, but the packet does not show them opened.

### 2. On the world view, earthquakes and plate boundaries stop dead at a straight line across the map
- **Place:** 06-map-opened.png, lower third of the map (below Central America and south-east Asia).
- **What is there:** All dots and white plate lines end along one horizontal line. South America, southern Africa, Australia and the southern oceans show nothing: no Andes earthquakes, no boundary around the Pacific's south side. The map picture is also a box with white bands either side.
- **Why it matters to me:** The core idea of this lesson is that earthquakes trace plate boundaries all the way round the Pacific. If the map opens like this on my projector, students see "no earthquakes south of the equator" and half the Ring of Fire is missing. I know enough to know that is wrong, but I could not explain why the map shows it.
- **How serious:** Stops the lesson if it is real; minor if the screenshot was simply taken before the layers finished drawing.
- **Suggested change:** **Needs checking in the live app.** If it is slow drawing, show a clear "layers still loading" sign so a teacher waits before talking. If the data really is cut off, fix the map.

### 3. Nothing says what is measured data, what is a calculated estimate, and what is a drawn simplification
- **Place:** 06-map-opened.png and 08-tool-layers.png (layer names); the "i" button beside the map title; "Map's description" on the card in 05-open-search.png.
- **What is there:** Five layers: "Earthquakes (Last 30 Days)", "Historic Major Earthquakes", "Other causes of earthquakes", "Tectonic Plate Boundaries", "Global Active Earthquake Faults". The only source information on screen is the small credit strip at the bottom ("... Peter Bird, Hugo Ahlenius/Nordpil | U.S. Geological Survey / PAGER"). The walk-through did not open the "i" button or "Map's description", so I cannot tell what they say.
- **Why it matters to me:** Earthquake locations are instrument records. Plate boundaries are a scientist's interpretation drawn as a line; real boundaries are often wide zones. A thick, crisp white line teaches "a crack you could stand on". I need one plain sentence per layer: who made it, is it recorded or interpreted, what years, what size of earthquake is included.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson (I would have to research each layer myself before using it).
- **Suggested change:** In each layer's information button, give a two-line plain note: "Recorded by seismometers (USGS), updated ..." or "Interpreted boundary, simplified to a line (Bird, 2003)". **Needs checking in the live app** what the "i" buttons already say.

### 4. The 30-day earthquake layer will lead students to think the western United States has the most earthquakes
- **Place:** 06-map-opened.png (grey dots packed over Alaska and the western US, few elsewhere) and 07-map-popup.png ("1 of 17" at one click near San Francisco).
- **What is there:** A dense cloud of small grey dots over the US and Alaska, with far fewer small dots anywhere else in the world. The pop-up example is a magnitude 2.
- **Why it matters to me:** I believe this is because the US has many more instruments, so tiny earthquakes get recorded there and not elsewhere, but I am not a geoscientist and nothing on screen confirms it. Students will read it as "America shakes most". This is exactly the kind of cause-and-effect misconception I check for.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson; creates a misconception if not addressed.
- **Suggested change:** State in the layer note what the smallest recorded magnitude is inside and outside the US, or give a one-click "magnitude 4.5 and above" view so the world is compared like for like.

### 5. The pop-up: unexplained terms, no time of day, and "1 of 17"
- **Place:** 07-map-popup.png and 08-tool-layers.png, the white pop-up in the middle.
- **What is there:** "2 Magnitude Earthquake", "1 km depth", "November 14, 2026", "12 km SE of Livermore, CA, USA (38, -122)", "USGS PAGER ALERT:", "No PAGER Issued", and "1 of 17". In 07 the top of the pop-up is a blank white strip with only "1 of 17" in it.
- **Why it matters to me:** "PAGER" is never explained; students will ask and I do not know. "No PAGER Issued" sounds like something went wrong. There is no time and no time zone, so I cannot talk about "how long ago". "(38, -122)" is rounded to whole degrees, which puts the point many miles from "12 km SE of Livermore". "1 of 17" means my one click picked up 17 things, and I cannot tell students which dot I am describing. Depth and magnitude are given with no hint of how certain they are ("1 km depth" for a tiny earthquake looks like a default or rough value to me, but I cannot tell).
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson.
- **Suggested change:** Replace the PAGER lines with plain words ("Damage alert level: none issued") or drop them for small earthquakes; add the time with its time zone; show coordinates to two decimals or not at all; when several things are under the click, list them by name. The pop-up text comes from the map, so this may be a map fix, not an app fix.

### 6. The filter histogram has no labels and no units, and starts at "-0.1"
- **Place:** 10-layer-filter.png, Filter panel on the right.
- **What is there:** "Show features where", "Depth", "is at least", "Enter a number", "Histogram", a bar chart with only "-0.1" at the left end and "675.4" at the right end, and "Showing all 949 features".
- **Why it matters to me:** The shape is good science: most large earthquakes are shallow, with a small bump of very deep ones, and I would love to use it. But the chart has no "kilometers", no count scale and no axis title, so students cannot read it as a graph, which is a skill my standards ask for. A depth of "-0.1" (above the ground?) will get a question I cannot answer. "features" is not a word my students or I use for earthquakes. The table for the same layer does say "Depth (kilometers)" (11-layer-table.png), so the unit is known to the app.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson.
- **Suggested change:** Label the axis "Depth (kilometers)" and the bars "number of earthquakes"; say "Showing all 949 earthquakes" or "949 items"; show the unit beside "Enter a number". **Needs checking in the live app** whether the map redraws as the handle is dragged or only after "Apply"; for a projected demonstration, live redraw is what makes it work.

### 7. "Historic Major Earthquakes" does not say what counts as historic or major, and the times have no time zone
- **Place:** 11-layer-table.png, table under the map; 08-tool-layers.png, layer list.
- **What is there:** "Historic Major Earthquakes (Total: 949 | Selection: 0)", columns "Date and Time", "Depth (kilometers)", "Magnitude", "Place". Rows such as "April 21, 1977 at 4:24 AM ... 7.5". The rows are not in date order (1977, 1977, 1977, 1995).
- **Why it matters to me:** A different map's card says "magnitude of 7.0 or higher ... between 1950-2023" (05-open-search.png), but this layer does not state its own cut-off or years where I can see them. Students comparing "949 in history" with "last 30 days" need to know they are comparing magnitude 7 and above over decades with everything down to magnitude 2 over a month. "4:24 AM" where? If the app converts to my computer's time, that is worth saying.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson.
- **Suggested change:** Put the range in the layer name or its note ("magnitude 7+, 1950 to 2023"), state the time zone in the column heading, and open the table sorted by date.

### 8. Elevation profile: the exaggeration is stated well, but the chart and the source line still leave gaps
- **Place:** 18-profile-drawn.png and 19-profile-hover.png, panel under the map.
- **What is there:** "Vertical exaggeration ×6.4" and, in the notes, "Heights are drawn 6.4 times as tall as distances." Also "Highest 10,607 ft", "Lowest 3,950 ft", "Total climb and descent for lines up to 31 mi", and "Elevation data. Sources: Vantor, Airbus DS, USGS, NGA, NASA, CGIAR, GEBCO, N Robinson, NCEAS, NLS, OS, NMA, Geodatastyrelsen and the GIS User Community".
- **Why it matters to me:**
  - Stating the exaggeration is exactly what I want, and the plain sentence is better than the "×6.4" label. But I only see that sentence in the notes; in the screenshot I see just "Vertical exaggeration ×6.4". **Needs checking** whether the sentence is visible on screen or only read aloud by a screen reader.
  - The left scale starts somewhere near 3,000 ft, not at sea level, and that is not marked. Together with the 6.4 times stretch, a gentle plateau looks like jagged mountains. Students will say "that's a cliff".
  - 6.4 appears to be whatever fits the box. **Needs checking**: if it changes with each line and with the panel height, two profiles cannot be compared by eye, and there is no way shown to set it to 1 for a true-shape view.
  - "Total climb and descent for lines up to 31 mi" reads like a broken sentence. I think it means "not worked out for lines this long", but it does not say so.
  - The source line is a list of organisation names. It does not tell me this is measured elevation, how fine the detail is, or whether "10,607 ft" to the nearest foot is believable for a 120-mile line. To a student, a number to the foot looks exact.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson (minor for a quick demonstration, more so if students write down values).
- **Suggested change:** Show the sentence under the chart; mark the bottom of the scale ("scale starts at 3,000 ft") or offer "start at sea level"; offer a "true scale (×1)" button; reword to "Total climb and descent: only for lines shorter than 31 mi"; replace the source list with one plain line ("Measured elevation from satellite and survey data, blended by Esri; detail varies by place") and keep the names behind a "more" link; round the highest and lowest values.

### 9. The Measure tool could not be tried in the walk-through, so I cannot judge it
- **Place:** 16-tool-measure.png, Measure panel; notes line "measure-distance FAILED".
- **What is there:** "Distance", "Area", "Coordinates", "Clear", "Units km mi", "Choose a tool, then click on the map. Double-click to finish." The test could not press "Distance" (it waited for the button to become ready and timed out). No measurement result appears anywhere in the packet.
- **Why it matters to me:** Measuring the distance from an earthquake to a plate boundary or a city is an obvious student task. I cannot tell whether the tool gives true distances over the curved Earth, which matters on the flat world view in 01-first-open.png where Greenland looks as large as Africa and the scale bar ("2,000 km") can only be right at one latitude.
- **How serious:** Cannot tell. **Needs checking in the live app**: does the button work, and is a long distance (say Tokyo to San Francisco, about 8,300 km or 5,100 mi) reported correctly?
- **Suggested change:** If the button was genuinely stuck, fix it. Add one line in the panel: "Distances follow the curve of the Earth" (if true).

### 10. The 3D view shows bare ground with no earthquakes, no labels, no scale, and no word on exaggeration
- **Place:** 22-to-3d.png, whole screen.
- **What is there:** After 45 seconds, a tilted view of land and the curve of the Earth. No earthquake dots, no plate lines, no place names, no scale bar. Nothing says whether the ground heights are true or stretched. (The 2D picture before it was also zoomed in on Utah where few dots were in view, so I cannot be sure the layers are missing or just not in the picture.)
- **Why it matters to me:** What I hoped for from 3D on an earthquake map is depth: earthquakes getting deeper under a continent at a trench. The packet does not show that, and the "1 km depth" in the pop-up is not shown as depth anywhere. With no labels or scale, students cannot say what they are looking at or how big it is. If the hills are stretched in 3D and nobody says so, that is the same scale misconception the profile is careful to avoid. And 45 seconds of blank screen is a long time in a ten-minute demonstration, though the facts say the test machine had no graphics card.
- **How serious:** Slows the lesson; I would leave 3D out of the demonstration as it stands.
- **Suggested change:** **Needs checking on a real school Chromebook**: how long 3D takes, and whether the earthquake layers appear. State in 3D whether heights are true scale. Keep a scale or a distance cue on screen.

### 11. A layer description teaches "plates floating on the mantle"
- **Place:** 05-open-search.png results list (text recorded in the notes for the "Global Tectonic Plates" card).
- **What is there:** "Earth's lithosphere is composed of plates floating on the mantle."
- **Why it matters to me:** "Floating" makes students picture rafts on a liquid. The mantle is solid rock that flows very slowly. This is one of the most common misconceptions I have to undo, and here it is in the catalogue text.
- **How serious:** Minor for the app (it is the map's description), but it is the sort of line that makes me doubt the rest.
- **Suggested change:** Reword to something like "plates that move slowly over the hot, solid but slowly flowing mantle beneath".

### 12. Layer list: names greyed out while ticked, an unexplained layer, and no help anywhere
- **Place:** 08-tool-layers.png, Map layers panel.
- **What is there:** "Global Active Earthquake Faults" is ticked but its name is grey, like the two unticked layers. "Other causes of earthquakes" is off, has a dot on its filter icon, and no explanation. Rows of small unlabelled icons under each layer. The facts say there is "no teacher how-to guide and no help link inside the app".
- **Why it matters to me:** I cannot tell whether the faults layer is on, off, or only shows when zoomed in. "Other causes of earthquakes" is intriguing (mining? reservoirs? volcanoes?) but I would not switch on something I cannot explain. With nine icons per layer and no guide, I would stick to tick boxes only.
- **How serious:** Minor to slows the lesson.
- **Suggested change:** If a layer only draws when zoomed in, say "zoom in to see this layer". Give "Other causes of earthquakes" a one-line description. A one-page "run this as a ten-minute demonstration" sheet for this map would cover most of my "what do I need to know that I don't" questions.

## What works well for me

- No account, no install, opens on a Chromebook, and typing "earthquake" finds a ready-made guided lesson map in two clicks.
- Real, current data: the pop-up shows an earthquake dated the same day, with magnitude, depth and place, credited to the USGS.
- The elevation profile states its vertical exaggeration as a number and in a plain sentence, and the pointer reads off "59 mi along: 7,985 ft" while marking the same spot on the map.
- The depth histogram and table for the historic layer show a real pattern (most large earthquakes are shallow) that students could discover for themselves.

## Would I use it?

I would use it for a ten-minute projected demonstration only after checking the map draws completely, and the single change that would matter most is a working legend that says what each colour, size and line means and whether it is recorded data or a simplified drawing.
